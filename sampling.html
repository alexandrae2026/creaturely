sampling & temperature

Why the same question can return a different answer.

The previous entry showed how each cycle of inference ends: the model produces a probability distribution for the next token, and one token is chosen from it. This page looks more closely at how that choice can be made.

Inside it is one important part of the answer to a question most people ask sooner or later: why does the same question, asked twice, sometimes come back differently? In many LLM systems, the next token is not simply the one with the highest probability. It is drawn from a distribution.

i · greedy decoding and sampling

There are two common ways to choose a token from a distribution. The first is to take the most likely token. This is called greedy decoding. In principle, when the model, context, and settings remain the same, greedy decoding follows the same path each time: the same words in produce the same words out.

The second is to draw from the distribution as a lottery draws from weighted tickets. A token with a large share of the probability is more likely to be drawn, but a token with a smaller share may still be chosen. This is called sampling, and many text-generating systems use some form of it.

Press sample enough times and the tally begins to resemble the bars: water most often, dark sometimes, sea now and then. Any one draw cannot be predicted from the probabilities alone, but across many draws their proportions begin to appear.

This is one way in which an LLM is and is not predictable. The distribution shapes what is more or less likely, while sampling allows different paths through it. It is one reason that regenerating an answer can produce another variation rather than a copy. In a system that uses sampling, a new draw is made each time a token is chosen.

ii · temperature

Before the draw, one setting can reshape the whole distribution. It is called temperature. Each token’s score is divided by the temperature before the scores are converted into probabilities, a conversion performed by a function called softmax.

A low temperature sharpens the distribution, moving more of the probability toward the front-runners until sampling behaves almost like greedy decoding. A high temperature flattens it, giving less likely tokens a greater chance of being drawn. The dial below performs this calculation in miniature.

Temperature is often described as a creativity setting, and it is easy to see why. At 0.2, the line will end in water nearly every time. At 2.5, the sentence might find the sea, or the dark, or somewhere stranger.

It is worth being precise about what the setting does. Temperature does not add ideas, moods, or intentions. It rescales scores the model has already computed. A higher temperature gives more probability to alternatives that previously held smaller shares.

A wider field of possible choices may produce language that feels more surprising, but it may also produce language that is less coherent. Temperature changes the amount of variation in the draw; it does not measure creativity.

Sampling is also one of the routes by which a fluent falsehood can arise. A draw can commit to a token that was possible but wrong, and the loop then continues coherently from it, since every later token is predicted from a context that now includes the error. Lowering the temperature reduces this kind of variation but does not make the distribution truthful; the most likely continuation is only the most likely one.

The model produced the scores beneath the distribution by processing the text already in context through parameters learned during training. The next entry introduces how those parameters were shaped.

Part of the poems’ capacity to surprise their maker may live here: in the draw made at each new token. The distribution shaped what was likely, while sampling left room for another choice.

If the most likely token had always been chosen, the generation would in principle have followed the same path each time, provided that the model, context, and settings remained the same.

a note on the visualizations

The visualizations in this glossary are approximations. They were made to illustrate the concepts and are not actual representations of what happens inside a working model. Their purpose is to help bridge a gap between the complexity of an LLM’s inner workings and human experience.

notes on this page

The candidate probabilities on this page were set by hand and carried over from the invented step in inference. The sixth bar gathers all remaining tokens into elsewhere. The temperature arithmetic, however, is the real operation in miniature: the scores are divided by the temperature and then converted into shares by the softmax.

Working systems may combine temperature with cutoffs that this page leaves aside. In top-k sampling, only a fixed number of the most likely tokens remain in the draw. In top-p sampling, the surrounding system keeps the smallest group of likely tokens whose probabilities together reach a chosen threshold. The remaining tokens are excluded before sampling.

A temperature setting of zero conventionally means greedy decoding; the temperature calculation itself cannot divide scores by zero. Even greedy decoding is not always perfectly repeatable in practice, since the model, surrounding context, system settings, or numerical details of the computation may vary between runs.

The surrounding system applies the decoding settings its provider has chosen, and most people using a chat service are not shown exactly what those settings are.
