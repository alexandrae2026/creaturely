tokens & embedding

What does a Large Language Model (LLM) see when you type?

This is an interactive glossary that accompanies the project creaturely. The aim of the glossary is to provide an introduction to how LLMs work, with a specific interest in the processes that make them act in ways that are similar to what we think of as being creative – surprise, experimentation, innovation, and the unexpected.

The sentence that passes from entry to entry in this glossary is “the echo of a beam never sent,” which comes from the first code poem of creaturely, “photonic emergence.”

When you type a sentence to an LLM, it feels as if the model is reading and responding to it. However, before an LLM can do anything with what you write, the sentence must be translated into a form it is designed to use.

First, it is cut into pieces. Each piece is assigned a number, and then each number is used to retrieve a long list of values which is called an embedding. Each value is simply a number, such as 0.82, −0.4, etc.

These three steps: 1. tokenization, 2. numbering, and 3. embedding, give the LLM its first numerical representation of what you wrote. This first entry of the glossary is an attempt to show what that process looks like.

i · tokens

An LLM does not read letters; instead, it works with tokens. Tokens are pieces of text cut from the sentence you wrote in the chat. A token may be a whole word, part of a word, a punctuation mark, or even a single character. Dividing the text into these pieces is called tokenization.

The cuts are governed by a fixed vocabulary and a set of rules established before the LLM itself was trained.

Training is a long process in which an LLM encounters a very large collection of text and gradually adjusts its internal numbers, becoming better at predicting how a sentence continues. By the time most of us interact with an LLM, that training is over. While a model’s responses may change according to the context you provide, its learned parameters, the numbers set during training, ordinarily remain fixed. A later entry in the glossary describes the training process more fully.

Try the tokenizer below. Type anything. Type your name.

the cutter

Notice where the cuts fall. Common words and recurring sequences of characters often stay whole, while less common ones are divided into smaller pieces. Your name, unless it is familiar to the tokenizer, may break into fragments.

On this page, the small dot at the beginning of a token represents a space. Spaces belong to the cuts as well. A piece that begins with a space usually begins a new word; one without a space may continue the word before it. For example, to an LLM, “ light” and “light” can therefore be two different tokens, each with its own number.

A vocabulary is built by looking for recurring patterns in a large collection of writing. In one common form of tokenization, the process begins with very small units and repeatedly combines those that often appear together. Frequent combinations gradually acquire entries of their own, while rarer words and sequences are assembled from smaller pieces.

An LLM’s vocabulary is therefore a record of what recurs in the writing that was used to build it and the decisions made by its makers.

ii · numbering

The vocabulary assigns each token a number, known as a token ID. After cutting your sentence, the tokenizer returns a row of these token IDs. Press show the numbers above and look again. Wherever the same tokenizer is used, the same piece receives the same number. Another tokenizer may divide the sentence differently and use an entirely different set of numbers.

The number is an identifying label; it does not measure or define the token. It simply tells the LLM which list of values to retrieve in the next step. Think of a wall of numbered drawers, one for every entry in the vocabulary. The token ID opens one drawer, and inside is that token’s list of values, learned during training. The ID itself carries nothing; it only says which drawer.

This helps explain a once-famous error. Ask a language model how many r’s appear in the word strawberry, and some models may respond incorrectly. That may seem strange for a machine built from language. But look at what the model is actually given.

strawberry

s t r a w b e r r y

three r’s, plainly visible.

three token IDs. no separate letters to count.

For the model, strawberry arrives as three numbered pieces rather than a row of separate letters. If an LLM answers this question correctly, it is probably drawing on patterns it was taught related to the spelling of words during training, or using an additional tool to examine the word. This is important because it encourages us to imagine that the model sees language like we do. But, its actual encounter with language begins somewhere else, after language is transformed into tokens.

iii · embedding

Token IDs do not carry meaning on their own. A token ID functions more like a location. At that location, an LLM finds a long list of values, hundreds or even thousands of them, which together form a token’s embedding. These values provide a token with a starting point in a mathematical space that has many dimensions, like coordinates on a map that cannot be fully pictured. In GPT-3 175B, for example, each embedding had 12,288 dimensions.

LLMs learn these locations during training. Because they are developed through patterns of use, tokens that appear in related contexts often acquire similar embeddings and occupy regions that are close in the space. This nearness or proximity can reflect a similarity in meaning, grammar, tone, or use.

This embedding is only a starting point. As a sentence passes through an LLM, the initial representation of each token is altered by its relationship to the tokens around it. Later entries in the glossary will help explain how this happens, and how the meaning or significance of the same token can shift from one sentence to another.

iv · costs

Tokenization has economic consequences. The more tokens a passage contains, the more computing time and memory it will generally require, and if a commercial service charges by the token or “usage,” the more it will cost. While a model processes all the tokens in your prompt together, it usually generates its response one new token at a time.

Because the vocabularies are shaped by the texts used in training, they also do not divide all languages equally. Many widely used tokenizers divide English into relatively few pieces while cutting other languages into many more. The same passage can therefore require substantially more tokens, and cost more to process, in one language than in another.

one sentence, six languages

Same meaning, different token counts. The construction of the vocabulary helped decide which languages travel cheaply through these systems.

So, what does an AI model see when you type? The sentence you wrote becomes a sequence of token IDs. Each token ID is exchanged for an embedding: a position in a multi-dimensional space learned during its training. This is the first translation.

Everything else in the glossary happens after these initial three steps.

a note on the visualizations

The visualizations in this glossary are approximations. They were made to illustrate the concepts and are not actual representations of what happens inside a working model. Their purpose is to help bridge a gap between the complexity of an LLM’s inner workings and human experience.

notes on this page

The tokenizer on this page is made with a small, hand-built vocabulary of a few hundred pieces. It uses a simple longest-match rule and falls back to individual characters when it cannot find a longer piece. Real tokenizers use much larger vocabularies and different methods, so their tokenization will differ from these examples. Frequently recurring sequences are nevertheless more likely to remain whole, while less common strings are more likely to be divided into smaller pieces.

Real embeddings have hundreds or thousands of dimensions. The constellation here uses three hand-placed dimensions to suggest spatial relationships, while the comparison panel shows eight illustrative values. Its positions and values demonstrate the idea of regions or neighbourhoods but they were not taken from an actual language model.

The token counts shown for the six languages are also illustrative, rather than produced by an actual tokenizer, so the comparison is schematic. Unequal tokenization across languages is documented, but which languages require more or fewer tokens, and by how much, varies according to the tokenizer, language, writing system, and passage.
