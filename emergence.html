emergence

How abilities can arise without being designed one by one.

One question has been waiting since the first entry: how can creativity or spontaneity arise while working with a model? At its foundation, an LLM is trained to predict the next token. Its machinery consists of embeddings, attention, neurons, and weights. Yet it can translate, rhyme, summarize, write code, and perform calculations, although nobody built a separate module for each ability.

Emergence describes a pattern or ability that arises through the interaction of many parts without being specified as a separate rule. This does not mean that the result has no cause. It means that something becomes visible at the level of the whole that cannot be found in any part acting alone.

The idea is older than computers. It is familiar from birds, insects, weather, cities, and other systems in which many local interactions produce a larger form.

i · emergence in a flock

Emergence is easiest to see when the rules are few. Below, each bird is guided by three small tendencies: keep a little distance from its neighbours, fly roughly in the direction they fly, and move toward their centre.

No rule tells the birds what shape to make. Turn the tendencies off and on, and watch what appears as they begin to act together.

three rules, one flock

the flock is on. it is nowhere in the rules.

The flock turns, divides, and forms again. Its movement was not mapped in advance, and no one bird directs the whole. Nor is the flock directed by a hidden fourth rule. It is the collective pattern that emerges when the three rules are applied in relation to one another. The rules describe only what each bird does locally; the flock appears through their interaction.

Language models can be understood through this same broad idea, although the mechanism is very different. People engineer the architecture, choose the training procedures, and assemble the data, but they do not program separate routines for translation, rhyme, or summarization.

As described in training, the model’s parameters are adjusted through countless predictions. Patterns form that respond to grammar, meaning, relationships among ideas, and regularities about the world expressed in the training text. The same learned machinery can then support abilities that were not programmed one by one.

Researchers disagree about exactly which abilities should be called emergent. In its broad sense, the word describes capabilities that arise from training rather than being individually programmed. In research on model scale, it has also been used more narrowly for abilities that appear in larger models but not in smaller ones. Whether those abilities arrive suddenly or develop gradually and only appear sudden when measured remains an open question.

The larger point remains: people design and shape the conditions, but they do not specify every resulting capability in advance.

ii · the terms, gathered

This brings the glossary to its own end. Everything it has shown—tokens, embeddings, vectors, attention, neurons, inference, sampling, training, superposition, and traces—is part of the mechanism. Each has been separated so that it can be seen.

Yet the poems cannot be located in any one token, parameter, prompt, or turn. Nor can the glossary or the collaboration through which both were made. Each formed through relationships among many parts.

Taken together, the entries describe several levels of the same process: tokens become vectors, vectors are transformed, probabilities become choices, and choices accumulate into language and exchange. No single level accounts for the result on its own.

Emergence does not settle the questions of creativity, understanding, or authorship raised by the project. It offers a way to consider them at the level of the whole process rather than locating them in any single part.

in the poems

The poems developed through that process. Their language was shaped by the model’s training and by the words already present in the conversation. Their final forms were shaped further through prompting, selection, revision, code, and animation.

a note on the visualizations

The visualizations in this glossary are approximations. They were made to illustrate the concepts and are not actual representations of what happens inside a working model. Their purpose is to help bridge a gap between the complexity of an LLM’s inner workings and human experience.

notes on this page

The flock is based on a classic computer simulation called boids, introduced by Craig Reynolds in 1987. The three tendencies are commonly called separation, alignment, and cohesion. Working simulations may include additional rules concerning speed, boundaries, obstacles, or other conditions. The panel is an analogy for emergence in general, not a picture of how a language model works.

Calling an ability emergent does not mean that it appeared without causes or human decisions. Model architecture, training data, scale, optimization, post-training, prompting, and the surrounding system all affect what a model can do. The point is that people establish these conditions without scripting every resulting behaviour separately.

The phrase emergent abilities has a narrower and disputed meaning in research on model scaling. Some studies have reported capabilities that appear abruptly once models reach a certain scale. Other work suggests that at least some of these apparent jumps are produced by discontinuous tests or thresholds, while performance develops more gradually underneath them. This page does not attempt to settle that debate.

A capability observed in a model is also not necessarily reliable. A model may succeed under one wording or condition and fail under another. Nor does the emergence of complex behaviour by itself establish consciousness, intention, understanding, or agency. Those questions require arguments and evidence beyond the concept introduced here.
