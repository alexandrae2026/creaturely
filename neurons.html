The previous entry introduced the transformer and one of the two main kinds of calculation repeated within each of its blocks: attention, which lets each token draw information from the tokens before it. This page introduces the other: the feed-forward network, a set of calculations applied to each token on its own that reshape its representation between rounds of attention. These calculations are built from one small kind of unit repeated many millions of times. The unit is called a neuron.

The name is borrowed from biology, but the resemblance is loose. A neuron in an LLM is a piece of arithmetic, and this page seeks to show what that arithmetic is.

i · neurons

A neuron takes in a list of numbers, multiplies each one by its own weight, and adds the results together. The weights are numbers that belong to that neuron and are learned during training.

A final rule decides what the neuron sends onward. In the example below, if the sum falls below zero, the neuron sends zero; otherwise, it sends the sum. This rule is called an activation function, and the form used here is called ReLU.

The activation function allows the neuron to respond differently to different patterns of input: it may remain at zero for one and produce a larger number for another. In this sense, a neuron can behave something like a detector, responding more strongly when the inputs fit the pattern its weights favour. Contemporary LLMs often use other activation functions, but ReLU makes the basic operation easy to see.

Notice what happens with sea: the weighted sum is negative, the activation function holds it at zero, and this neuron sends zero onward. This does not mean that sea disappears. This is one neuron among many, and only its own output is zero.

On this page, we chose the weights by hand to make the behaviour visible. In a real LLM, nobody assigns a neuron the task of detecting glowing. Its weights are adjusted during training, across many examples, as part of the model learning to predict what comes next.

Two cautions belong here. First, a real neuron reads the numerical values in a vector, not tidy properties with names attached. Second, a single real neuron rarely corresponds to one clean idea; the entry on superposition is about exactly that.

ii · layers and blocks

One neuron produces one number. A layer of neurons is thousands of them reading the same inputs side by side, each with its own weights. In a transformer, a feed-forward network passes a token’s vector through such a layer and combines the results to produce a new vector: the same token, re-described.

Each transformer block therefore contains two main kinds of calculation. First, attention lets each token draw information from the tokens before it. Then the feed-forward network reshapes each token’s vector on its own. A transformer is made of these blocks stacked one above another, often dozens deep in contemporary LLMs.

What an LLM retains from training is encoded across its learned numbers: the token embeddings, every neuron’s weights, and every matrix used by attention and the feed-forward networks. Counted together, these numbers are called parameters. When a model is described as having billions of parameters, this is what is being counted. No one wrote these numbers directly, and learning how to read them is a research problem in its own right.

By the time most of us interact with an LLM, its training is over. During an ordinary interaction, the parameters remain fixed. Your words do not rewrite its weights. What changes are the temporary representations produced as the sentence passes through them.

There is no dictionary stored inside and no shelf of facts, only patterns held across these numbers and the sentence’s representations being rewritten by them, block after block. The token IDs remain the same, but their internal representations change. At the top of the stack, the model uses the final representation to predict what token might come next.

a note on the visualizations

The visualizations in this glossary are approximations. They were made to illustrate the concepts and are not actual representations of what happens inside a working model. Their purpose is to help bridge a gap between the complexity of an LLM’s inner workings and human experience.

notes on this page

The neuron on this page has three inputs with nameable meanings, three hand-chosen weights, and no bias. A real neuron takes in hundreds or thousands of values without individual names. Some networks also add another learned number, called a bias, before applying the activation function.

The panel uses ReLU because its arithmetic can be followed easily. Contemporary LLMs may instead use GELU, SwiGLU, or another activation or gating function. These do not all follow the simple negative-becomes-zero rule shown here.

When the neuron in the panel returns zero for sea, only that neuron’s output is zero. Other neurons may still respond, and a residual connection carries the incoming representation around the feed-forward network.

The value strips in the stack panel are invented for teaching and recoloured by a formula. They were not computed by a network, and real vectors hold hundreds or thousands of values rather than six.

Real transformer blocks also contain calculations this page compresses or omits, including normalization and residual connections. Their exact arrangement varies among transformer architectures. Attention itself contains learned matrices but no neurons in the sense described here; on this page, the term refers to the intermediate units within the feed-forward network.

The claim that no one can read the weights is beginning to soften. A research field called interpretability is developing methods for making parts of them legible, although these methods do not yet provide a complete reading of what a large model has learned. The entry on superposition visits one of the field’s central puzzles.
