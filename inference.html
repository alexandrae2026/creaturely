inference

How an LLM produces its reply, one token at a time.

speak into the field
the field is still

The glossary so far has followed a sentence inward: divided into tokens, given vectors, adjusted by attention, and rewritten block by block through the stack. This page follows the reply outward.

Using a trained model to produce an output is called inference: the model reads your words and produces new ones. A reply may be long, but it is generated through one small move repeated again and again. The model predicts what token could come next, one token is chosen, and the process begins again.

i · next-token prediction

Inference runs as a loop. The model works from everything available so far within its context window: your words and its own words alike. At the top of the stack, the final representation of the most recent position is used to produce a score for every token in the vocabulary. These scores are often called logits.

The scores are then converted into a probability distribution. Every possible next token receives a share of the probability, and the shares add up to one. One token is chosen from that distribution and added to the sequence. The loop begins again, now working from one token more.

This way of producing text, in which each new token is generated from the tokens before it, is called autoregressive. It is one reason an LLM writes as it does: one small commitment at a time, each one changing the context for the next.

one token at a time

Watch the counter under the candidates. Without shortcuts, every cycle would send the growing sequence through the stack again. Working systems usually store some of the results already calculated for earlier tokens so that they do not need to repeat all of that work. Only the newest token needs a new pass through the stack, although its attention can still draw on stored information from the tokens before it. As the sequence grows, this requires increasing amounts of memory and computation.

Notice also that the model did not retrieve a finished sentence stored somewhere inside it. The line is assembled one token at a time. Until the last token is produced, the complete sentence does not exist.

Nothing in this loop checks a claim against the world. At every step the model produces the token that best continues the text according to its learned patterns, and a fluent continuation is not the same as a true one. When a model states something false with the same confidence as something true, the behaviour is commonly called hallucination, although some researchers prefer confabulation, since nothing is perceived. The mechanism this page describes is enough to produce it: prediction rewards plausibility, the context may lack what the answer needs, and the loop commits to each token before the sentence is complete.

ii · the context window

The loop works from everything available so far, but “everything” has an edge. The entry on attention introduced the context window: the stretch of tokens the model can attend to at one time. In a conversation, your turns and the model’s turns fill that window together, along with any instructions or other material supplied by the surrounding system: the software around the model that assembles its input, applies its settings, and returns its output to you.

When the window is full, the surrounding system must stop, shorten, or replace some of what it supplies. In the simplest arrangement, the oldest tokens are left out of the next turn. The model does not archive them for itself. A surrounding system may save, summarize, or retrieve earlier material, but it must place that material back into the context before the model can use it again.

the window is nearly empty.

This helps explain a common frustration. One reason a model may appear to forget something said much earlier is that the earlier words are no longer inside its context window. The model can also fail to use information that remains within the window. It does not choose to forget. It works only from the context it is given and the patterns it learned during training and now holds in its parameters.

Just as important, ordinary inference does not rewrite those parameters. Temporary representations and stored calculations are created as the model produces its reply, but they do not become new training or alter its learned weights. The weights remain fixed. Your words change the context and the reply produced from it, but they do not retrain the model.

The model’s weights therefore keep no record of having answered or interacted with you. Within a conversation, the context window carries the exchange forward. A chat service may also save the conversation or store memories outside the model and supply them again as context later. The entry on stigmergy returns to this distinction.

a note on the visualizations

The visualizations in this glossary are approximations. They were made to illustrate the concepts and are not actual representations of what happens inside a working model. Their purpose is to help bridge a gap between the complexity of an LLM’s inner workings and human experience.

notes on this page

The candidate tokens and their probabilities in the loop were set by hand, and the completed line is this page’s invention rather than the output of a working model. A real step scores a large vocabulary containing tens of thousands of tokens or more. A model may end its reply by producing a special end token; the surrounding system may also stop generation because it reaches a length limit or another stopping condition.

The window panel greatly compresses the scale. Real context windows are far larger, and their sizes vary widely among models. The context may also contain material the panel does not show, including system instructions, documents, images, tool results, or other information supplied alongside the conversation.

A chat service may add processes outside the model, such as saved conversation histories, summaries, retrieved notes, or memories. These do not ordinarily become part of the model’s learned weights. The system supplies them as context when it wants the model to use them.

Working systems also commonly use a key-value cache. During generation, this cache stores keys and values already calculated for earlier tokens so that they do not need to be recomputed at every step. The cache grows as new tokens are produced and is part of the model’s temporary working state, not a permanent memory of the conversation.
