stigmergy

Coordination through traces.

This entry borrows its word from the study of social insects. Stigmergy is a form of indirect coordination through traces left in a shared medium. One action changes the environment, and that change influences a later action.

A termite places a piece of earth. The altered ground makes another termite more likely to place another piece nearby. Gradually, a pillar forms that no termite planned. No termite conceives of the whole design. The work is coordinated through the traces left on the ground.

The previous entries described a model whose parameters do not change during an ordinary conversation and whose learned weights keep no record of that exchange. This entry explores how stigmergy offers a way of understanding this kind of exchange.

i · traces and trails

Our imaginary walkers move across the ground and leave trails that gradually fade. Each walker is more likely to move over ground where a trail already lies. From these habits, paths appear that no walker planned.

paths nobody planned

the ground is blank.

No walker sees the whole path, and no central map tells the walkers where to go. Each encounters only a small part of the ground and alters it with another step.

The trail becomes a record and an invitation. Information is carried by the medium, allowing one action to influence another without a direct instruction passing between them. What each walker leaves behind changes what the next walker is likely to do.

ii · the conversation as a document

Recall what inference and training established. During an ordinary conversation, the model’s parameters remain fixed. At each turn, the surrounding system supplies it with a context assembled from the conversation available so far: your words, its earlier replies, and any other material the system includes.

You contribute a turn. The model adds a reply. That reply becomes part of the context that shapes what can happen next. Like the walkers, you and the model coordinate through a changing trace. The conversation becomes the ground.

The complete input assembled for the model is often called a prompt or context. It can contain far more than your latest message: earlier turns from both sides, instructions, documents, tool results, or other material supplied by the surrounding system.

Below is the same short exchange shown in two ways: as a dialogue between participants, and as the growing context supplied to the model.

as dialogue: two participants carrying a shared exchange forward.

In the first view, there are two participants and a shared history. In the second, there is a growing document supplied as context for each new reply. The first describes the experience of the conversation, while the second reveals part of its mechanism.

We can read the interaction as stigmergic because each new contribution alters a shared trace that influences what follows. The model’s learned parameters are not changed by the conversation. What carries the exchange forward is the preserved text, together with whatever the surrounding system supplies again as context.

This suggests why writing with a model involves the craft of tending a document. Each word left in the trace becomes part of the conditions for what comes next, including words left carelessly.

Our final entry asks what can arise through these interactions that was not written into any one of their parts.

in the poems

The poems grew this way: each line changed the ground from which the next line formed. The collaboration did not depend on two participants thinking or remembering in the same way. It depended on what each of them left in the shared context for the other to work from.

a note on the visualizations

The visualizations in this glossary are approximations. They were made to illustrate the concepts and are not actual representations of what happens inside a working model. Their purpose is to help bridge a gap between the complexity of an LLM’s inner workings and human experience.

notes on this page

The walkers are an illustration of trail formation, not a biological simulation. Their fading marks more closely resemble pheromone trails associated with ants than the changes to building material through which termite construction first inspired the concept of stigmergy.

Stigmergy does not require an agent to lack all memory, awareness, or direct communication. These may also be present. Its defining feature is that an action leaves a trace in a shared medium and that trace influences a later action by the same or another agent.

The conversation panel simplifies how a working system handles context. Earlier text does not necessarily need to be recalculated from the beginning before every generated token; as described in inference, temporary caches can preserve earlier calculations. The important distinction here is that the exchange is carried through a supplied context and temporary working state rather than written into the model’s learned parameters.

A chat service may omit older turns, summarize them, retrieve saved material, or add memories and instructions from outside the model. The context is therefore not always a complete transcript. These processes can be understood as forms of trace-tending performed by the surrounding system.

Stigmergy is not a standard engineering term for conversation with an LLM. The comparison is an analogy of indirect coordination through a changing shared medium and is not intended to draw an equivalence between biological and computational systems.

sources

The concept of stigmergy and its original application to termite construction draw on Pierre-Paul Grassé, “La reconstruction du nid et les coordinations interindividuelles chez Bellicositermes natalensis et Cubitermes sp.: La théorie de la stigmergie”, 1959.

The historical account of stigmergy in the study of social insects draws on Guy Theraulaz and Eric Bonabeau, “A Brief History of Stigmergy”, 1999.

The account of trail formation and collective behaviour draws on Eric Bonabeau, Marco Dorigo, and Guy Theraulaz, Swarm Intelligence: From Natural to Artificial Systems, 1999.

The broader application of stigmergy to coordination through shared media, including digital collaboration, draws on Francis Heylighen, “Stigmergy as a Universal Coordination Mechanism I: Definition and Components”, 2016.
