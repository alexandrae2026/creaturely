creaturely: a glossary · ii

vectors

How meaning becomes geometry.

The first entry of the glossary showed how a sentence is divided into numbered pieces, and how an LLM gives each piece a starting position in a space with many dimensions. This page introduces two things those positions make possible. First, the vectors of two tokens can be compared to calculate how similar they are. Second, in some embedding spaces, one vector can be subtracted from another so that the relationship between them becomes a direction that can sometimes be applied elsewhere.

These positions are fixed starting points, but this does not mean that a token has one fixed meaning. As a sentence moves through a contemporary LLM, the representation of each token changes in relation to the tokens around it. The next entry introduces attention, one of the main processes through which this happens.

i · similarity

A token’s coordinates, taken together, form its vector. A vector is an ordered list of numbers. When those numbers locate a token within an embedding space, the vector describes a position. When one vector is subtracted from another, the resulting vector describes the change from one position to the other.

During training, the values within these vectors are repeatedly adjusted. Tokens that occur in similar linguistic contexts tend to develop similar patterns of values, while tokens whose uses overlap less tend to develop less similar patterns. A similarity calculation compares these learned patterns numerically.

Similarity does not mean that two words have the same meaning. It measures a relationship between the ways they appeared in the training texts. Select any two words below to compare them.

On this page, the token examples are whole words so that each word can be represented by a single vector.

These positions preserve patterns of relationship found in the training texts. A dictionary gives us definitions written to explain the meanings of words. An embedding does something different: it records patterns in how words were used.

In the teaching example on this page, beam and light occupy similar positions to represent their overlapping patterns of use. Water and song are placed farther apart to represent a less direct relationship. Once the positions have been established, their similarity can be calculated from the numbers in their vectors.

Embedding systems commonly make this comparison through a calculation called cosine similarity. Picture each vector as an arrow drawn from the centre of the space to the word’s position. Cosine similarity ignores how long the two arrows are and asks how closely their directions agree. Two arrows pointing almost the same way receive a high score, even if one reaches much farther than the other. Two arrows at right angles receive a score near zero.

ii · vector arithmetic

A second kind of relationship appears when, instead of comparing the positions of two words, we calculate the difference between them. Subtracting the vector for the first word from the vector for the second produces another list of numbers. Each number records how much one coordinate must change to move from the first position to the second. Taken together, these changes form a direction through the many-dimensional space.

The new vector therefore records a relationship rather than a position. In some embedding spaces, pairs of words that share a similar relationship produce steps that point in roughly the same direction.

The teaching space below uses the step from seahorse to sea to represent the movement from a creature to its associated environment. The interactive asks what happens when the same step begins with another creature.

the step from one word to another

In this teaching space, adding the creature-to-environment step to bird produces a position nearest to sky. Adding the same step to moth produces a position nearest to night. The arithmetic does not ordinarily land exactly on the vector of another word. It produces a position, and the word whose vector lies closest to it is found.

The step from moth to flame forms a direction too, but it describes another kind of relationship: attraction rather than environment. Because the two directions were calculated from different relationships, one cannot simply stand in for the other.

The same mathematical step can be added to any vector, but the result will not always make sense. If the creature-to-environment step begins at sea, the arithmetic still produces a position, although that position does not lie near a word with any recognizable connection to the relationship. The calculation has continued to work, but the analogy has not.

In a real embedding, a recurring direction can emerge when related pairs of words have appeared in similar patterns of use. This technique became famous through an early word-embedding system called word2vec. Researchers found that the step from man to woman, when applied to king, produced a position close to queen. A relationship between words had become an approximately linear direction within the embedding space.

The technique became known as vector arithmetic: subtracting one vector from another to describe a relationship, then adding that difference somewhere else to see whether the relationship repeats.

iii · inherited patterns

Researchers studying these early embeddings found something else. In a widely used word2vec embedding trained on Google News, applying the step from man to woman to computer programmer produced a position near homemaker. Applying the step from father to mother to doctor produced a position near nurse. Social stereotypes present in the training text had become part of the geometry.

It is important to remember what these directions record. They are not discoveries about the world, but patterns learned from a particular body of writing: a collection shaped by who produced it, what was preserved, and what its makers chose to include. The assumptions and inequalities carried by that writing can therefore become part of the relationships the model learns.

Current models are trained and evaluated differently, and developers may use filtering, further training known as fine-tuning, and other methods intended to reduce harmful associations. These measures do not make the problem disappear. The underlying principle remains: the geometry is learned from patterns of usage, not from an independent account of what is true.

Follow a direction, and you are following a path of written history.

A vector is not simply a way of storing a token as a point. It gives the model a starting position from which relationships can be compared, similarities measured, and, in some embedding spaces, directions traced.

At this stage, however, we have described only the starting representation of each separate token. The model has not yet interpreted your sentence as a sentence. The next entry introduces the process we experience as “reading,” when the representation of each token begins to be shaped by the tokens around it.

a note on the visualizations

The visualizations in this glossary are approximations. They were made to illustrate the concepts and are not actual representations of what happens inside a working model. Their purpose is to help bridge a gap between the complexity of an LLM’s inner workings and human experience.

notes on this page

The teaching spaces on this page use three hand-placed dimensions in place of the hundreds or thousands used by real embeddings. Their slow turning is presentational; real models compare vectors across all their dimensions at once.

The similarity numbers in the interactive are calculated from straight-line distance within this teaching space. Embedding systems commonly use cosine similarity, which compares the angle between vectors rather than the distance between their endpoints. The interactive therefore illustrates the general idea of similarity without reproducing that calculation.

Cosine similarity is one method for comparing embeddings. It is not, by itself, the process through which a contemporary LLM interprets a sentence. As the tokens pass through a transformer, their representations change according to their context. The next entries introduce this process.

The creature-to-environment directions were drawn by hand so that the arithmetic would land cleanly. In a real embedding, vector arithmetic works approximately and unevenly, and its results depend on both the model and the writing used to train it.

The word2vec examples involving king and queen, computer programmer and homemaker, and doctor and nurse are documented outputs from systems studied during the 2010s; they were not invented for this page. Later research has questioned whether analogy completions, by themselves, provide a reliable measure of bias. Other forms of analysis have nevertheless demonstrated that social associations and inequalities can be learned from language corpora and reproduced within word embeddings.

Early systems such as word2vec assigned one stable vector to each word. Contemporary LLMs usually begin with token embeddings and then repeatedly alter the representation of each token according to its context. Vector arithmetic offers an important illustration of relationships within learned spaces, but it is not a complete account of how a contemporary language model interprets or produces a sentence.
