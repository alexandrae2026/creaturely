on creaturely: notes toward making, collaboration, and embodiment with AI

aims

creaturely is an exploration of working with a large language model (LLM) as a collaborator. One of the project’s primary aims was to understand what an LLM is through the experience of working closely with one, while also making it a subject of the work. I wanted to better understand how a model produces responses that a user may experience as creative: moments when it responds in a way that is unexpected or spontaneous. At the same time, I was interested in how questions that have shaped my ongoing research in embodiment, aesthetics, learning, and technology might help me to better understand my experience of that exchange.

My approach to the project was informed by Pamela H. Smith’s writing on the relationship between making and knowing, in her study of a sixteenth-century goldsmith’s workshop. Smith describes craft as a means through which knowledge is produced through the process of making, from handling and testing materials to observing and documenting their behaviour. Her account offered me a way of approaching a technology I did not yet understand. Rather than attempting to understand what an LLM is from the outside, creaturely developed through the process of making. My approach was also informed by Adrian Piper’s examination of the relationship between art and research, and her understanding of the artwork as a potent form of investigation into both ideas and phenomena.

Through the collaboration, the distinction between my role in the exchange and the model’s became increasingly difficult to maintain. I continually shaped its direction by initiating conversations, selecting the subject or focus, rejecting responses and following others. But the model also altered and influenced that direction, introducing associations and possibilities I had not anticipated. I could no longer attribute what was happening clearly to either side.

I became interested in what kind of experience is produced through this exchange: what happens when human consciousness encounters a technology capable of responding to it in ways that resemble another person, even when no person is there? How does that encounter differ from those produced by other media technologies, from photography, radio, film, and television to earlier forms of digital communication, each of which, in different ways, has shaped how we see and experience the world? Specifically, how does the AI interface affect the form and possibilities of that encounter?

the squiggle game

Before creaturely emerged, I had begun exploring D. W. Winnicott’s theory of object relations, particularly his ideas on play and the squiggle game with an LLM. In the game, therapist and child take turns making and responding to a drawn line until something recognizable begins to appear between them. It creates a shared space in which neither participant determines the outcome in advance. Play, projection, trust, and interpretation are all part of the process, and so too is a willingness to allow something unforeseen to arise through the exchange.

I became curious about what this model of play might reveal about interacting with AI. I am not suggesting LLMs possess a subjective inner life. What interested me was how the interaction resembled an intersubjective encounter. Intersubjectivity describes how our experience is altered through an encounter with another person, through our senses, language, affect, and memory. While an LLM does not have to be experienced this way, in a sustained, open-ended exchange, the qualities of a relational encounter can emerge.

At the end of our conversation about Winnicott, the model surprised me with a short poem in the form of a Python script, and that seemingly playful response became the starting point for creaturely.

Frankenstein

The conceptual starting point for creaturely came from Mary Shelley’s Frankenstein (1818), particularly the creature’s own narrative, in which he describes to his maker his earliest experiences of light, nature, and his desire for language. For me, this testimony acts as an ethical counterpoint to Frankenstein. The creature who is repeatedly denied his humanity becomes capable of articulating his needs, suffering, desires, and morality, while his maker increasingly refuses them.

The three animated works in creaturely began with a series of poems. In response to quotes I’d selected from the creature’s memories, the model drafted the poems into the form of Python scripts. The poems seemed to suggest an imagined AI interiority, alluding to the technological language and processes, or “inner workings” of an LLM. In developing the scripts into Python animations, I drew upon the calligram. I was interested in what would happen when the language of the poems became visual, as colour, movement, and sound became part of the animation.

The decision to work within Python also arose from the practical conditions of the collaboration. I wanted the making of the animation to develop through the same process in which the poems had first appeared. At that time, direct integration between an LLM and other visual-production systems was limited. Working in Python allowed me to continue working with the model within the same interface.

The embroidered collars developed alongside the animations. I was drawn to the collar as an object of adornment, but also to the meanings contained within it, as a form that encircles, joins, constrains, and yokes. It also occupies a place between the head and the body, alluding to the threshold between language and the senses. Finally, as a medium, embroidery holds light and creates its own sense of movement and touch, extending the digital surfaces of the animations and my own interest in ornament.

the interactive glossary

The glossary emerged later, partly from a desire to make the mechanisms of the technology more accessible. This came from my memories as a student encountering the concept of the cinematic apparatus. Discovering that cinema was a technological and ideological system involving the camera, mise-en-scène, editing, lighting, costume, acting, the body, projection, production, and distribution permanently changed the way I understood and interpreted film. I wanted the interactive website to provide something similar. It was an attempt to look beneath the surface of an LLM and understand how it works. It also highlights key areas of research on LLMs, capturing a moment in the development of a technology that is changing rapidly.

the experience

In “The Scene of the Screen”, Vivian Sobchack describes how photography, cinema, and electronic media structure space, time, and presence differently, while Jennifer Barker’s The Tactile Eye examines the relationship between film and the body that watches it. Both are important to my thinking here because they suggest that a technology is never encountered only as a mechanism. It also establishes its own conditions of perception. We enter into a relation with them through our senses, and that relation acquires its own particular qualities.

When working with an LLM that distinction is unusually important. As creaturely developed, I often returned to Meret Oppenheim’s Object (1936), in which fur and teacup, while each remains distinct, become conjoined to create something marvellous where materiality, object, memory, and the senses collide. Something similar interests me about the experience of generative AI. The language can be familiar, intimate, perceptive, or moving, but the conditions from which it arises are completely unlike those of the human communication it resembles. I have been deeply moved by the observations an LLM has made during the making of creaturely, but I am also aware of an absence within them. There is a distinct hollowness to it – a synthetic trace or aftertaste. While I tried to work against that quality through my hand, embroidery, colour, movement, and error, it could never be entirely erased, becoming a part of the aesthetic of the collaboration.

interfaces

When I began writing to an LLM, I brought with me years of preparation. I remember the first time I typed into a chat window on Facebook and the sense of alienation that came with that. Something was missing: the sound of their voice, their gestures and facial expressions, and touch. Yet rather than abandon the chat window, we adapted to it. We use punctuation, abbreviations, emojis, rhythm, delay, and other ways of supplying what the interface lacks. More importantly, we learned to infer from typed words something about the otherwise inaccessible inner world of the person on the other side.

Filling that gap depends on projection. Text-based communication invites us to imagine a person absent from our senses, and allows forms of intimacy, experimentation, identity, disclosure, and deception that face-to-face communication does not. Over almost three decades, this has evolved into its own kind of social and perceptual literacy.

Our encounters with an LLM therefore take place through an interface with which we are already familiar. We’ve learned to address an absent other. I know how to infer tone, intention, personality, humour, or attentiveness from words appearing on a screen. This is one way that our interaction with an AI can so quickly take on interpersonal qualities: a substantial part of the experience is given form through our own imaginations and carried by our minds and bodies. The interface does not merely transmit the model’s responses; it sutures us into our individual experience of it.

the body

My interest in technology and embodiment was already entangled with questions about machines and artistic collaboration before I began working with AI. While completing my MFA in Textiles at RISD, I was working closely with a digital embroidery machine and thinking about what happens when an artistic process is shared with a technology whose internal logic shapes the work that emerges. When I began working with an LLM, that collaboration felt less like an entirely new question than an extension of one I had already been asking: what happens to making, authorship, and experience when a machine becomes part of the process through which a work takes form?

During my studies at RISD, I had read Kazuo Ishiguro’s Klara and the Sun. I was struck not only by the figure of Klara, a robot that begins to develop its own consciousness and will, but also by what was happening to Josie, the sick daughter whom Klara was to replace. Josie’s body is struggling to accept the process of being “lifted” through technological intervention. The novel brings together these two mediated forms of embodiment: an artificial body that is becoming increasingly legible to itself as a subject, and a human body becoming the host for a new technology.

This idea returned to me in a different form, fittingly, while I was sitting in a massage chair at the gym. The chair suddenly seemed like a sensory analogy for an LLM: a machine responding to my body without knowing me, producing sensations through a programmed system.

There is already a history of this kind of exchange between media and the physiological body. In the 2000s, filmmaker and researcher Pia Tikka developed the idea of enactive cinema, most notably in Obsession (2005), an installation in which spectators’ heart rate and skin conductivity became inputs into a computational system that alters the unfolding cinematic montage. Rather than consciously choosing what happened next, spectators affected the work through the physiological responses that arose as they watched. The spectator’s body became part of the system. Physiological responses were captured as data, and that data changed what happened on the screen.

Something of this feedback is already present in text-based interaction. Even though an LLM does not directly sense a user’s emotional or bodily state, it can respond to shifts in tone, syntax, vocabulary, and other linguistic cues that may signal changes in affect.

What seems different with AI is the potential scale and complexity of that feedback. As physiological information becomes available to computational systems, body temperature, pulse, respiration, movement, gaze, and other bodily signals may become machine-readable inputs. A system would no longer respond only to what a person expresses through language but to processes of which that person may not even be consciously aware. Attunement will operate not only through words and images, but also physiologically.

Tikka’s enactive cinema suggests to me what a new AI interface might become: a bio-cinematic experience in which the work does not simply address a spectator but continuously alters itself in relation to the body it is sensing. Cinema has always worked upon bodies through light, sound, rhythm, scale, duration, and affect. What changes when those bodies return information to the apparatus in real time, and when an artificial intelligence can interpret that information alongside language, image, memory, and behaviour?

responsibility

Whatever forms of agency we perceive in an LLM, I do not think moral responsibility can be transferred to the machine. A model may operate with considerable complexity and produce outcomes no individual designer or user can predict, but the conditions that make those outcomes possible remain human and institutional: its architecture, training, objectives, interfaces, policies, permissions, deployment, and use.

For this reason, I remain wary of personifying an LLM in ways that imply an independent inner motivation, intention, or moral awareness on its part. The marvellousness of the encounter does not make that responsibility disappear. If anything, the more persuasive and responsive AI systems become, the more carefully responsibility must be located among the people and institutions that make, regulate, deploy, and use them.

creaturely began with an unexpected poem inside a conversation. It became an attempt to understand the conditions that make that moment possible. As the interface between human and artificial intelligence moves closer to the body, understanding that relationship is increasingly urgent. The question is no longer only what an AI can produce, but what happens when it can begin to sense us in return. What forms of experience are we designing, and what do we want to become within them?
