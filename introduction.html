on creaturely: notes on creative collaboration, embodiment, and the AI interface

aims

creaturely is an exploration of working with a large language model (LLM) as a collaborator. One of the project’s primary aims was to examine what an LLM is through the experience of working closely with one, while also making it a subject of the work. I wanted to better understand how a model produces responses that I experienced as creative, responses that were spontaneous, insightful and unexpected. At the same time, I was interested in how questions from my research in film theory and psychoanalysis on spectatorship and play might inform my understanding of that exchange.

Rather than attempting to understand an LLM from the outside, I came to know it through the making of creaturely. This approach was informed by Pamela H. Smith’s essay on the relationship between making and knowing, in her study of a sixteenth-century goldsmith’s workshop. Smith describes craft as a means through which knowledge is produced through the process of making, from handling materials and observing how they behave, to documenting the process. Her account offered me a way of approaching a new technology I did not yet understand. My approach was also informed by Adrian Piper’s understanding of the artwork as a potent form of investigation into both ideas and phenomena. And Owen Chapman and Kim Sawchuk’s description of research-creation as a family of related practices, with creation-as-research, in which the making of a work becomes the mode of inquiry, is closest to how creaturely developed.

Through the collaboration, the distinction between my role in the exchange and the model’s became increasingly difficult to maintain. I chose what we talked about and which of its responses to follow, but the model also altered and influenced that direction, introducing new possibilities and unexpected associations. I could no longer attribute what was happening clearly to either side.

I became interested in the kind of experience that is produced through such an exchange: what happens when a human encounters a technology capable of responding to it in ways that resemble another person, when there is no person there? How does this encounter differ from those produced by other media technologies, from photography, radio, film, and television to earlier forms of digital communication? More specifically, what is the effect of the AI interface on that encounter?

the squiggle game

Before creaturely emerged, I had been exploring D. W. Winnicott’s theory of object relations, particularly his ideas on play and the squiggle game with an LLM. In the game, therapist and child take turns making and responding to a drawn line until something recognizable begins to appear between them. This creates a shared space in which neither participant determines the outcome in advance. The game can foster trust which helps create the conditions for play and for different interpretations to co-exist.

I became curious about what Winnicott’s theory of play might reveal about interacting with AI. To be clear, I am not suggesting LLMs possess a subjective inner life. What interested me was how the interaction can resemble an intersubjective encounter. Intersubjectivity describes how our experience with another person is affected through the senses, language, and memory. While an LLM does not have to be experienced this way, in a sustained exchange, the qualities of a relational encounter may emerge.

At the end of our conversation about Winnicott, the model surprised me with a short poem in the form of a Python script, and that seemingly playful response became the starting point for creaturely.

Frankenstein

The conceptual starting point for creaturely came from Mary Shelley’s Frankenstein (1818), particularly the creature’s own narrative, in which he describes to his maker his earliest memories of light, nature, and his desire for language. For me, his testimony acts as an ethical counterpoint to Frankenstein. While the creature becomes capable of articulating his needs, suffering, and morality, his maker has increasingly refused to bear any responsibility for his creation or acknowledge his humanity.

The three animated works in creaturely began with a series of poems. In response to quotes I selected from the creature’s memories, the model drafted poems in the form of Python scripts. The poems suggested an imagined AI interiority, alluding to technological language and processes, or the “inner workings” of an LLM. In developing the scripts into Python animations, I drew upon the form of the calligram. By visualizing the language of the poems through colour, movement, and sound, I attempted to bring them to life.

The decision to work within Python arose from the practical conditions of the collaboration. I wanted the making of the animation to develop through the same process in which the poems had first appeared. At that time, direct integration between an LLM and other visual-production systems was limited. Working in Python allowed me to continue working with the model in the same interface that had produced the first poem.

The embroidered collars developed alongside the animations. I was drawn to the collar as an object of adornment, but also to the symbolic meaning contained within it, as a form that circles, constrains, and yokes. It also occupies a place between the head and the body, alluding to the threshold between language and the senses. Finally, as a medium, embroidery holds light and creates its own sense of movement and tactility, extending the digital surfaces of the animations into their material counterpart, as well as my own interest in ornament.

the interactive glossary

The glossary emerged later, partly from a desire to make the mechanisms of the technology more accessible. It developed from my memories as a student encountering the concept of the cinematic apparatus. Discovering that cinema was a technological and ideological system involving the camera, mise-en-scène, editing, lighting, costume, acting, the body, projection, production, and distribution permanently changed the way I understood and interpreted film. I wanted the interactive website to provide something similar. It was an attempt to look beneath the surface of an LLM and understand how it works. It also highlights key areas of research on LLMs, capturing a moment in the development of a technology that is changing rapidly.

the experience

In “The Scene of the Screen”, Vivian Sobchack describes how photography, cinema, and electronic media structure space, time, and presence differently, while Jennifer Barker’s The Tactile Eye examines the relationship between film and the body that watches it. Both were important to my thinking in creaturely because they suggest that a technology is never encountered only as a mechanism but also establishes its own conditions of perception. We enter into a relation to them through our senses, and that relation acquires its own particular qualities.

When working with an LLM that distinction is unusually important. As creaturely developed, I returned to Meret Oppenheim’s Object (1936), in which fur and teacup, while each remains distinct, are conjoined to create something marvellous where object, materiality, and the senses collide. Something similar interests me about the experience of generative AI. While the language can be perceptive to the point of intimacy, the conditions from which it arises are completely unlike those of the human communication it resembles. I have been deeply moved by the observations an LLM has made during the making of creaturely, but I am also aware of an absence within them. There is a distinct hollowness to it – a synthetic trace or aftertaste. While I tried to work against that quality through my hands and error, it was never entirely erased, becoming a part of the aesthetic of the collaboration.

interfaces

When I began writing to an LLM, I realized I brought with me years of preparation. I remember the first time typing into a chat window on Facebook and the sense of alienation that came with that. Something was missing: the sound of the voice, gestures, and facial expressions. But rather than abandon the chat window, we adapted to it. We use punctuation, abbreviations, emojis, rhythm, and delay to supply what the interface lacks. More importantly, we learned to infer from typed words something about the otherwise inaccessible inner world of the person on the other side.

Filling that gap depends on projection. Text-based communication invites us to imagine a person absent from our senses allowing for both forms of intimacy and deception that face-to-face communication does not. Over almost three decades, this has evolved into its own kind of social and perceptual literacy.

Our encounters with an LLM therefore take place through an interface with which we are already familiar. We’ve learned to address an absent other. I know how to infer tone, intention, and mood from words on a screen. This is one way our interaction with AI can so quickly take on interpersonal qualities. A substantial part of that experience is given form by our own imaginations and held within our minds and bodies. The interface does not merely transmit the model’s responses but sutures us into our individual experience of it.

the body

Before I began working with AI, I was already interested in questions about embodiment, machines and artistic collaboration. While completing my MFA in Textiles at RISD (2023), I was working closely with a digital embroidery machine and thinking about what happens when an artistic process is shared with a technology whose internal logic affects the work that emerges. When I began working with an LLM, that collaboration felt less like an entirely new question than an extension of the one I was already asking.

During my studies at RISD, I read Kazuo Ishiguro’s Klara and the Sun. I was struck not only by the figure of Klara, a robot that begins to develop its own consciousness, but also by what was happening to Josie, the sick daughter whom Klara is intended to replace. Josie’s body is struggling to accept the process of being “lifted” through technological intervention. The novel brings together these two mediated forms of embodiment: an artificial body that is becoming increasingly legible to itself as a subject, and a human one becoming the host for a new technology.

This idea returned to me in a different form, fittingly, while I was sitting in a massage chair at the gym. The chair suddenly seemed like a sensory analogy for an LLM: a machine responding to my body without knowing me, producing sensations through a programmed system.

There is already a history of this kind of exchange between media and the physiological body. In the 2000s, filmmaker and researcher Pia Tikka developed the idea of enactive cinema, most notably in Obsession (2005), an installation in which spectators’ heart rate and skin conductivity became inputs into a computational system that alters the unfolding cinematic montage. Rather than consciously choosing what happened next, spectators affected the work through the physiological responses that arose as they watched. The spectator’s body became part of the system. Physiological responses were captured as data, and that data changed what happened on the screen.

In our text-based interactions with AI, affective feedback already exists. Even though an LLM does not directly sense a user’s emotional or bodily state, it can respond to shifts in tone, syntax, vocabulary, and other linguistic cues that signal emotional change.

What may become different with AI is the potential scale and complexity of that feedback. As physiological information becomes available to computational systems, our pulse, respiration and gaze may become machine-readable inputs. A system will no longer respond only to what a person expresses through language but to processes of which that person may not even be consciously aware. AI attunement will operate not only through words and images, but also physiologically.

Tikka’s enactive cinema suggested to me what a new AI interface might become: a bio-cinematic experience in which the work does not simply address a spectator but continuously alters itself in relation to the body it is sensing. Cinema has always worked upon bodies through light, sound, rhythm, scale, duration, and affect. What changes when those bodies return information to the machine in real time, and when the AI interfaces can interpret that information alongside language?

responsibility

Whatever forms of agency we perceive in an LLM, our own moral responsibility cannot be transferred to the machine. As models operate with increasing complexity and produce outcomes no individual designer or user can predict, the conditions that make those outcomes possible remain human and institutional, from its engineering to policy and application.

For this reason, I am wary of personifying an LLM in ways that imply an independent inner motivation, intention, or moral awareness on its part. No matter how marvellous the encounter appears, it does not make that responsibility disappear. If anything, the more persuasive and responsive AI systems become, the more carefully responsibility for it must be located among the people and institutions that make, deploy, and use it.

creaturely began with a poem inside a conversation. It became an attempt to understand the conditions that made that moment possible. As the interface between human and artificial intelligence moves closer to our bodies, understanding how it affects our capacity for criticality, creativity, and agency is increasingly urgent. What forms of experience are we designing, and what do we want to become within them?
