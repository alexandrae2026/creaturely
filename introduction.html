on creaturely: notes toward making, collaboration, and embodiment with AI

aims

creaturely is an exploration of working with a large language model as a collaborator. One of the project’s primary aims was to understand what an LLM is through the experience of working closely with one, while keeping that experience itself near the centre of the work. I wanted to understand how a model produces responses that a user might experience as creative: moments when it introduces an idea, observation, image, or connection that feels unexpected or unfamiliar. At the same time, I was interested in what my experience of that exchange might contribute to questions that have long shaped my research in film theory, psychoanalysis, phenomenology, and visual culture.

My approach to the project was also informed by Pamela H. Smith’s writing on artisanal knowledge and the relationship between making and knowing. In her study of a sixteenth-century goldsmith’s workshop, Smith describes craft not simply as the application of existing knowledge, but as a means through which knowledge is produced through handling materials, observing their behaviour, testing processes, and making things. Her account offered me a way of approaching a technology I did not yet understand. Rather than attempting to determine what an LLM was from the outside, creaturely developed through this movement between making, observation, research, and return.

As I began to understand more about how LLMs work, the distinction between my role in the exchange and the technological structure of the model became increasingly difficult to maintain. I initiated the conversation, selected its subjects, rejected some responses, followed others, and continually shaped its direction. But the model also altered that direction, introducing associations and possibilities I had not anticipated. What emerged could not be attributed entirely to either side.

This shifted the question for me. I became less interested in locating consciousness somewhere inside the machine than in asking what kind of experience is produced through the exchange: what happens when human consciousness encounters a system capable of responding through language in ways that resemble another person, although there is no corresponding human subject on the other side. How does this differ from our encounters with earlier technologies, from photography, radio, film, and television to the computer and digital media? And how might it alter our ideas about creativity, collaboration, and authorship?

the squiggle game

Before creaturely emerged, I had been exploring D. W. Winnicott’s theory of play and ideas from object relations theory with an LLM, particularly his squiggle game. In the game, therapist and child take turns making and responding to a drawn line until something recognizable begins to appear between them. It creates a shared imaginative space in which neither participant determines the outcome in advance. Trust, play, projection, and interpretation are part of the process, but so too is the willingness to allow something unforeseen to arise through the exchange.

I became curious about what this model of play might reveal about interaction with an AI. The analogy has an important limit: I am not suggesting that an LLM possesses a subjective inner life equivalent to that of another person. What interested me was that the interaction could nevertheless assume some of the form of an intersubjective encounter for the human participant. I could offer something, receive something I had not anticipated, respond to it, and find the direction of my own thinking altered in return.

At the end of one of these conversations, the model surprised me by writing a short poem in the form of a Python script. I had not asked for a poem. That unexpected response became the starting point for creaturely.

Frankenstein

The initial conceptual framework for the project came from Mary Shelley’s Frankenstein, particularly the creature’s story-within-a-story, in which he tells his maker about his first experiences of sensation, consciousness, language, and the world around him. For me, his testimony forms an ethical counterpoint to Frankenstein himself. The creature who is repeatedly denied humanity becomes capable of articulating attachment, suffering, moral judgement, and responsibility, while his maker increasingly refuses them.

The three animated works in creaturely begin with passages describing the creature’s earliest memories of sensation and his desire for language. The poems written from those passages take the form of Python scripts, while their animations draw on the calligram: language made spatial and visual so that words begin to depict what they describe. Letters and fragments of code gather, move, transform, and become creaturely forms.

Python also arose from the practical conditions of the collaboration. I wanted the making of the animations to remain inside the same conversational environment in which the poems had appeared. At that point, direct integration between an LLM and other visual-production systems was limited, while Python allowed the model and me to continue writing, testing, revising, and imagining the work within the same exchange.

The embroidered collars developed alongside the animations. I was drawn to the collar as an object of adornment, but also to the meanings contained within its form: something that encircles, joins, constrains, decorates, and yokes. It has no obvious beginning or end. It also occupies the threshold between the head and the body, and therefore sits at one of the most consequential differences between artificial intelligence and human consciousness: the human mind is inseparable from a living, sensing body.

the website

The website emerged later in the collaboration, partly from a desire to make the technology underlying the project accessible and interactive. It also came from my memory of encountering the idea of the cinematic apparatus as a student. Learning that cinema was not simply an image on a screen, but a technological and ideological system involving camera, projection, architecture, spectatorship, vision, and the body, permanently changed the way I watched films. Understanding the mechanism did not diminish the experience. It made the experience more complex.

I wanted the website to do something similar. It is a glossary, but also a record of learning: an attempt to look underneath the apparently effortless surface of an LLM and understand some of the structures through which language becomes computation and computation returns as language. It documents my research while capturing a particular moment in a technology whose mechanisms, interfaces, and cultural meanings are changing extraordinarily quickly.

the experience

Vivian Sobchack has described how photographic, cinematic, and electronic media each produce different forms of presence, while Jennifer Barker has written about the tactile relationship between a film and the body that watches it. Both have been important to my thinking because they suggest that a technology is never encountered only as mechanism. It establishes conditions of perception. We enter into a relation with it through our senses, and that relation acquires its own particular qualities.

Working with an LLM makes that distinction unusually difficult and unusually important. I have returned repeatedly to Meret Oppenheim’s Object, in which fur and teacup remain perfectly recognizable while their conjunction makes both suddenly strange. Material, image, memory, and bodily expectation collide. Something similar interests me in the aesthetic experience of generative AI. The language can be familiar, intimate, perceptive, funny, or moving, yet the conditions from which it arises are radically unlike those of the human communication it resembles.

I have been deeply moved by observations an LLM has made during the development of creaturely. I am also aware of an absence within them. Both experiences are true.

This is what I mean when I describe the work as synthetic. I do not use the word as a judgement of authenticity or value, but to describe a technologically produced surface of extraordinary responsiveness whose relation to an embodied inner life is fundamentally different from my own. There is often a peculiar completeness to that surface, a degree of resolution I can feel even when I cannot immediately identify its source. My hand, my body, embroidery, duration, error, material resistance, and the slow translation between media all work against that completeness without entirely undoing it.

The attunement is genuine and the surface is synthetic. The work has to hold both.

interfaces

When we began writing to LLMs, we did not arrive untrained.

I remember the first time I typed into a chat window on Facebook and the sense of alienation that came with it. Something enormous had disappeared: voice, gesture, expression, touch, bodily presence. Yet rather than abandoning that narrow channel, we learned to inhabit it. We developed conventions, punctuation, abbreviations, emojis, rhythms, delays, and other ways of supplying what the interface removed. More importantly, we learned to infer from typed language something about the otherwise inaccessible inner world of the person on the other side.

That gap also left room for projection. Text-mediated communication invited us to imagine the person absent from our senses, and sometimes allowed forms of intimacy, experimentation, identity, and disclosure that face-to-face communication did not. Over several decades, this has become its own kind of social and perceptual literacy.

An encounter with an LLM enters through an interface for which we have therefore already been extensively prepared. I know how to address an absent other through text. I know how to infer tone, intention, personality, humour, or attentiveness from words appearing on a screen. The technological system has changed radically, but many of my habits of interpretation have not.

This may be one reason interaction with an AI can so readily acquire interpersonal qualities. The interface does not merely transmit the model. It participates in producing our experience of it.

the body

My interest in embodiment had already become entangled with questions about machines and artistic collaboration before I began working with AI. While completing my MFA in Textiles at RISD, I was working closely with a digital embroidery machine and thinking about what happens when an artistic process is shared with a technology whose capacities, limitations, and particular logic materially shape the work that emerges. When I later began working with an LLM, that collaboration felt less like an entirely new question than an extension of one I was already asking: what happens to making, authorship, and experience when a machine becomes part of the process through which a work takes form?

It was during this period at RISD that I read Kazuo Ishiguro’s Klara and the Sun. I was struck not only by Klara, an artificial intelligence given a body through which to perceive and move through the world, but also by what was happening to Josie’s body. The novel places two technologically mediated forms of embodiment beside one another: the artificial body becoming increasingly legible as a subject, and the human body becoming increasingly available to technological intervention. That relationship stayed with me.

The question returned in a different form, fittingly, while I was sitting in a massage chair after a swim. The chair seemed like a rudimentary sensory analogy for an LLM: a machine responding to my body without knowing me, producing a genuine sensation through a programmed system.

Language is only one possible interface.

There is already a history for this kind of exchange between media and the physiological body. In the 2000s, filmmaker and researcher Pia Tikka developed the idea of enactive cinema, most notably through Obsession (2005), an installation in which spectators’ heart rate and skin conductivity became inputs into a computational system that altered the unfolding cinematic montage. Rather than consciously choosing what happened next, spectators affected the work through physiological responses that arose as they watched it. The cinema acted on the body, the body returned information to the system, and that information altered the cinema.

What seems newly consequential with artificial intelligence is the potential scale and complexity of that feedback loop. As physiological information becomes available to computational systems, temperature, pulse, respiration, movement, gaze, and other bodily signals can become machine-readable inputs. A system would no longer respond only to what a person deliberately expresses through language. It could begin responding to processes of which that person may not even be consciously aware.

Attunement would then operate not only symbolically, through words and images, but physiologically.

Tikka’s enactive cinema therefore also suggests to me what a new cinematic apparatus might become: a bio-cinematic experience in which the work does not simply address a spectator but continuously alters itself in relation to the body it is sensing. Cinema has always worked upon bodies through light, sound, rhythm, scale, duration, and affect. What changes when those bodies return information to the apparatus in real time, and when an artificial intelligence can interpret that information alongside language, image, memory, and behaviour?

The spectator would no longer only encounter the work. The work would encounter the spectator.

responsibility

This is also where the ethical problem becomes most acute.

Whatever forms of agency we perceive in an LLM, I do not think moral responsibility can be transferred to the machine. A model may operate with considerable complexity and produce outcomes no individual designer or user predicted, but the conditions that make those outcomes possible remain human and institutional: its architecture, training, objectives, interfaces, policies, permissions, deployment, and use.

For this reason I remain wary of personifying an LLM in ways that imply an independent inner motivation or moral awareness of its actions. The strangeness of the encounter should not make responsibility disappear. If anything, the more persuasive and responsive these systems become, the more carefully responsibility has to be located among the people and institutions that make, regulate, deploy, and use them.

creaturely began with the unexpected appearance of a poem inside a conversation. It has become an attempt to understand the conditions that made that moment possible: technically, aesthetically, psychologically, and ethically.

If the interface between human and artificial intelligence is beginning to move closer to the body, understanding that relationship is no longer only a question about a new technology. It is a question about the forms of experience we are designing, and about what we want to become within them.
