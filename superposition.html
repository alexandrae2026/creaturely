superposition

How a model fits more features than it has dimensions to hold them.

The previous entry followed training as the model’s parameters acquired structure. The result, however, is not a system in which every learned pattern receives a separate place.

Interpretability researchers use the word feature for a recurring property or pattern represented in a model’s activity. A feature might respond to glowing, legal language, a sequence in code, or the letter that tends to follow q. Some are easy to name; many are not.

A model appears to represent more features than it has individual neurons or dimensions available to hold separately. One leading account of how they fit is called superposition: rather than giving every feature a private place, the model represents many of them through shared parts.

i · polysemantic neurons

The neuron introduced earlier was designed to respond to one tidy feature: glowing. The neuron below is deliberately less tidy. Feed it words from the glossary and watch its meter. It responds strongly to words about light, but it also responds to words about song.

This simplified behaviour is called polysemantic: one neuron participates in more than one feature. Researchers have found many neurons whose activity cannot be explained by one clear, nameable idea.

Try to complete the sentence “this neuron detects…” and the difficulty appears. Light is true, and song is also true, but neither answer describes everything the neuron does.

This does not mean that the neuron contains two complete meanings. A feature is usually represented through a pattern involving many neurons, while the activity of one neuron may contribute to several features. The question therefore begins to shift from “what does this neuron mean?” to “which patterns does it help represent?”

This sharing can work because many features are sparse: on a typical input, most of them are not strongly active. If features concerning light and song seldom become active at the same moment, they can use some of the same capacity with relatively little confusion. Sparsity makes the sharing possible, although it does not remove interference altogether.

ii · features in superposition

The same idea can be pictured through vectors. Imagine that each feature is represented by a direction in an activation space. If two directions are perpendicular, they can vary independently without being mistaken for one another.

A space with a thousand dimensions can contain only a thousand directions that are all exactly perpendicular. A model, however, may benefit from representing far more than a thousand features. When those features are sparse, it can pack additional directions into the space at angles. The directions are no longer perfectly independent, but the model can tolerate some interference when the features seldom become active together.

Training balances the usefulness of representing another feature against the interference that feature creates. Crowd the sphere below and watch the separation between its directions shrink.

features 3 active at once

The sphere makes the bargain visible. In three dimensions, only three directions can be mutually perpendicular. More directions can be added, but as the space becomes crowded, they become harder to distinguish.

A real model works in spaces with thousands of dimensions. These larger spaces can accommodate many more directions with far less crowding than the sphere suggests. Research on superposition proposes that neural networks take advantage of this geometry, allowing more features to be represented than the number of dimensions would otherwise permit.

This is one reason the parameters are difficult to read. Ask what one neuron means, and several features may answer at once. Ask where one feature lives, and it may be spread across many neurons. Interpretability is the field that tries to untangle these relationships. Recent methods attempt to separate shared representations into individual features again, with promising but partial results.

The parameters therefore hold patterns that their individual parts do not display cleanly. The next entry turns from the model’s internal representations to the conversation around it, and to a different kind of memory.

in the poems

In the poems, a single word often carries light, sound, movement, and memory at once. Inside the model, a single representation may likewise hold several features within the same shared parts.

The poems were written from a space in which meanings do not each have a private room.

a note on the visualizations

The visualizations in this glossary are approximations. They were made to illustrate the concepts and are not actual representations of what happens inside a working model. Their purpose is to help bridge a gap between the complexity of an LLM’s inner workings and human experience.

notes on this page

The neuron in the first panel is scripted to respond to two hand-chosen families of words. It illustrates polysemantic behaviour but does not reproduce the activity of a neuron found in a working model. Real mixtures are discovered through analysis rather than assigned in advance, and they can be far less easy to name.

There is no single, universally settled definition of a feature. On this page, the word describes a recurring pattern in a model’s activity associated with some property, context, or function. The names researchers give such patterns are interpretations based on the examples that activate them; the model does not attach those labels itself.

The sphere is a three-dimensional teaching image of a high-dimensional arrangement. Feature directions are not literal arrows stored inside a model. They are patterns described mathematically across many numerical values. In thousands of dimensions, many directions can remain nearly perpendicular, making the geometry far more forgiving than the sphere suggests.

Toy models show how the sparsity and importance of features can encourage superposition. Evidence from trained networks is consistent with this account, but the extent and organization of superposition in large language models remain active areas of research.

Methods called sparse autoencoders attempt to decompose a model’s activity into a larger collection of features, only a small number of which are active at one time. These methods have recovered many patterns that people can interpret, but they do not yet provide a complete or uniquely correct map of what a model represents.
