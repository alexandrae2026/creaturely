creaturely: a glossary · vii

training

How the parameters were learned.

This page seeks to introduce training: the process through which billions of numbers that begin mostly at random become the learned parameters of a working LLM.

Training is one part of how a model is made, and it takes place before an ordinary conversation begins. It helps answer a larger question: how is a model able to do what it does?

i · pretraining and loss

The first and longest stage is called pretraining. At its centre is one procedure repeated at an enormous scale.

The model is given stretches of real text and trained to predict the token that comes next. It produces a probability distribution over the possibilities, like the distributions introduced in inference. The actual next token in the training text becomes the target. The less probability the model assigned to that token, the higher its loss.

The loss is then traced backward through the model. A procedure called backpropagation calculates how changes to the parameters will affect it. An optimizer, usually based on gradient descent, uses that information to make very small adjustments intended to reduce the loss.

Then the procedure begins again, across many positions, many batches, and an enormous collection of text. The calculations are distributed across large clusters of specialized processors, such as graphics processing units (GPUs) or tensor processing units (TPUs). Prediction, measurement, adjustment: through this cycle, repeated again and again, the model’s parameters gradually cease to be random.

This is the engine of pretraining. The procedure does not require the model to understand the loss or hold it as a goal. It measures an error, traces it backward, and adjusts the numbers.

Through this repeated reduction of loss, token embeddings develop neighbourhoods, attention develops patterns, and the transformations inside the layers become responsive to recurring structures in the text.

Training provides us with an account of optimization, not evidence of motivation. When we describe a model as wanting to answer well, we are translating this optimized tendency into human terms. The individual predictions made during training are not stored, but their accumulated effects remain in the parameters.

ii · the embedding space

Training also helps answer where the embedding space comes from. Before training, each token’s vector typically begins at a largely random position: the space has no meaningful neighbourhoods. As the loss is reduced, these vectors are adjusted along with the rest of the model. Across training, tokens come to occupy positions that reflect patterns in how they appeared in the text.

In this panel, we placed those words by hand. In a working model, nobody assigns tokens their final positions. The positions emerge from the accumulated adjustments made during training.

They are not a complete record of everything the model encountered. They preserve compressed patterns in how tokens appeared across the training text. When pretraining ends, the space remains fixed unless another stage of training alters it. By the time an ordinary conversation begins, the training is over and the space no longer moves.

iii · post-training and fine-tuning

A pretrained model can continue text, but continuing is not the same as answering helpfully. A further set of stages therefore shapes how it responds. These stages are often gathered under the name post-training.

Post-training commonly includes fine-tuning on chosen examples of the kinds of response the model’s makers want it to produce. One influential further method is reinforcement learning from human feedback, or RLHF. People compare pairs of the model’s answers and judge which is better. A separate reward model is trained to predict those judgements, and the LLM’s parameters are then adjusted to produce answers that the reward model scores more highly.

The parameters are again changed through optimization, but the objective has shifted. The model is now being shaped not only by patterns in the training text, but by examples and preferences concerning how it should respond. Try being the judge below.

Your few clicks moved a meter. In a working process, many people may compare many replies. Together with written examples, policies, and other forms of evaluation, their judgements help shape the model’s manner: its helpfulness, its caution, its refusals, and its tone.

This is worth holding onto whenever a model seems to want something. Its behaviour may partly reflect tendencies reinforced during post-training: patterns of response that people, or other evaluators designed by people, rated more highly.

The parameters, then, were shaped in stages: first by patterns across a vast body of text, then by selected examples and judgements about how the model should respond. The next entry, superposition, returns to those parameters and to a puzzle about how many patterns they can hold at once.

a note on the visualizations

The visualizations in this glossary are approximations. They were made to illustrate the concepts and are not actual representations of what happens inside a working model. Their purpose is to help bridge a gap between the complexity of an LLM’s inner workings and human experience.

notes on this page

The training panel isolates one predicted token and repeats the adjustment immediately. Real pretraining calculates losses across many token positions and many examples at once, combines the resulting gradients, and then updates the parameters. Not every parameter changes by the same amount, and some may change scarcely at all during a particular step.

The settling space replays hand-placed final positions rather than learning them. Real training does not know the space’s destination in advance. Its structure emerges from the repeated adjustments made across many examples.

The preference panel is a teaching version of one part of RLHF, not a reproduction of its algorithm. Working systems combine several approaches to post-training. These may include supervised fine-tuning on written demonstrations, RLHF using a learned reward model, direct preference optimization without a separate reward model, and feedback generated by other models following written principles.

This page distinguishes optimization, which can be described through the training procedure, from motivation, which is an interpretation of the resulting behaviour. Whether richer accounts of agency or motivation are warranted remains a subject of research and debate. The final entry returns to that question.
